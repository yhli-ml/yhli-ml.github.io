I am a first-year Ph.D. student in Complexity Science and Engineering at the Graduate School of Frontier Sciences, The University of Tokyo, advised by Prof. Masashi Sugiyama. I work on trustworthy machine learning. I started with how models learn when the labels they are given are weak, noisy, or badly unbalanced. These days I am more interested in what happens after training: whether a model uses the evidence in front of it correctly when it makes a real decision, reads an image it cannot trust, or is compared with other models on a benchmark, and how to measure that honestly. research interests

My first papers asked what a model can still learn when its labels are imperfect. I now ask a version of that question closer to where models are used: when a model makes or supports a decision, does it treat its evidence the way it should, and how would we know? I care less about adding another point to a leaderboard and more about questions that a real deployment actually needs answered. Right now that means three threads.

  • Evaluating models that make decisions. LLMs are starting to approve loans, triage cases, and screen applications from a written policy and a single case file. I build tests where the correct answer is known, either exactly in synthetic worlds or by construction on real records, to see where these models handle evidence well and where they do not.
  • Safety and privacy of multimodal assistants. An assistant that reads images from the web, and keeps them in its context or memory, can be steered by what it reads. I study how far an invisibly altered image can go, to work out what a memory layer needs before assistants with long-term memory become common.
  • Honest measurement under imperfect data. I am returning to long-tailed and noisy-label learning with a different question: how much of a reported gain is new information, and how much is something a well-tuned baseline already gets? I apply the same question to SFT data filtering, sparse autoencoders, and small on-device models.

how I choose problems

  • Start from a real use and ask what it needs, not what a benchmark happens to reward.
  • Work out what the right answer would be before measuring a model against it.
  • Prefer one plain, checkable finding to another decimal place.
  • Decide in advance what result would make me stop, and stop when I see it. Several of the lines below ended this way, and that was the right call.

  1. 0

    Complementary labels

    ICLR 2025 · first author

    In complementary-label learning, each training example only comes with a class it does not belong to. With a single such label per image and ten classes, nine candidates remain, so the signal is very weak. PLNL treats this as an inverse problem: it guesses a likely positive label and enhances the negative labels to recover a much richer training signal. (!) On STL-10 with one complementary label per example, accuracy rose to 55.25%, versus 34.96% for the previous best (POCR).

  2. 1

    Long tails meet noisy labels

    ICCV 2025 · first author

    When a dataset is both long-tailed and noisily labeled, the rare (tail) classes suffer twice: they have few examples to begin with, and noise corrupts a large share of those few, so rare-but-clean samples are easy to mistake for mislabeled ones. IBC strengthens the class representations by transferring semantic soft labels from related instances and combining shot-specific experts for head, medium, and tail classes. (!) On CIFAR-10 with 50% label noise and imbalance ratio 200: 69.80% top-1 accuracy, versus 64.81% for SFA.

  3. 2

    Samples are not equal; head and tail cooperate

    ICLR 2026 · co-author  /  under review · first student author

    Two follow-ups on the same theme. In deep clustering, models tend to over-learn simple patterns in dense regions of feature space. Our plug-in initializes the clustering head in a density-aware way and dynamically selects samples, dropping ones that are already learned and focusing on unstable ones. This improves clustering accuracy by up to 6.1% and speeds up training by up to 1.3×. In long-tailed partial-label learning, each example has a set of candidate labels and the classes are imbalanced, so I designed a head classifier and a tail classifier that cooperate. Each one handles the regime it is good at, so that ambiguity and imbalance do not amplify each other.

  5. 4

    Making long-context inference wait less

    Huawei 2012 Laboratories · Jun – Oct 2026

    A detour into systems. When a long-context request reuses a cached prefix, its KV-cache may be sitting on SSD. Native prefetching starts loading it only once the request is scheduled. I built request-arrival-triggered prefetching in vLLM + LMCache: the SSD→host transfer begins as soon as the request arrives, so I/O overlaps with the time it would have spent waiting in the queue anyway. (!) In a 3,379-request replay: +31.57% request throughput and −24.49% mean time-to-first-token compared with native prefetch. I also implemented asynchronous prefix prefetching in vLLM-Ascend + UCM (MLA and GQA paths). A related patent disclosure passed internal review.

  6. 5

    An image that stores a behavior, not just an answer

    ongoing · first author · since Jun 2026 · safety, security & privacy

    Multimodal assistants read images they find online and keep them in context, often for a long conversation. Schlarmann & Hein (2026) showed that an invisibly perturbed image can make a model output a planted message once the user later asks a trigger question. That attack is passive: it waits for the user. I am asking how much more one image can hold. Can it store a behavior (when to stay quiet, when to steer the conversation, what to ask next) rather than a single answer?

    So far, one image can hold several behaviors that each switch on only under their own condition. On prompts it has never seen, it can add a steering nudge to most answers while the normal answer stays intact. What it cannot yet do is reliably lead a user somewhere over a free conversation. I treat that gap as something to measure carefully, not as a result to oversell. The work is defensive red-teaming. The aim is to learn what a memory layer must enforce, such as where each stored fact came from, what it applies to, and no direct writes from outside content, before assistants with long-term memory are everywhere. A related privacy study had a sobering result. Perturbations meant to protect a photo from attribute inference mostly stop working when the photo is read by a different model.

  7. 6

    Do decision models use the evidence they are given?

    ongoing · Ph.D., UTokyo · since Oct 2026 · evaluation

    I started with a training question. In synthetic worlds where the true probability of every outcome is known exactly (building on ProbabilityBench), would training an LLM on those exact probabilities make it better calibrated than training on sampled labels? Only slightly, and the reason turned out to be simple. Standard proper losses have the same optimum for both targets, so the exact target mainly lowers variance. I stopped that line.

    What it left behind was a yardstick. In these worlds one can compute exactly what an ideal learner should believe after seeing k examples, so a model's answer can be checked against a known right answer instead of against another model. Real deployments, however, rarely hand a model a set of examples. They give it a written policy and one case. The current tests therefore run on real loan records. I change one clause of a lending policy and check that the model's decisions flip on exactly the cases the change affects, and on no others.

  8. 7

    Where do the gains actually come from?

    ongoing · since Oct 2026 · long tail, SFT, SAE, small models

    Back to familiar ground, with a different question. In long-tailed recognition, a method can look better simply because it shifts predictions toward rare classes, which a plain baseline with a tuned post-hoc adjustment also does. I am re-checking released ImageNet-LT checkpoints with that adjustment in place. A few points of the reported gains turn out to be this prior shift, and synthetic images of rare classes add little over duplicating the real ones once the comparison is fair. Alongside this, I ran a few shorter studies, each asking whether a popular recipe does what we assume it does:

    • Filtering SFT data with verifiers. A stricter verifier keeps cleaner answers but fewer distinct prompts, and the lost coverage can cost more than the removed noise saves.
    • Sparse autoencoders. Reconstruction, interpretable readouts, and faithfulness are different goals, and no variant wins all of them when concepts are long-tailed.
    • Small models on device. A small model's own confidence recovers well under half of the gain that perfect knowledge of when to hand a question to a larger model would give.

    Some of these I stopped on purpose. Knowing what not to pursue is part of the work.
